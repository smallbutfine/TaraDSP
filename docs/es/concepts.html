1. What is Convolution?

Convolution answers one deceptively simple question:

"What does this sound like when played through that?"

That is genuinely all it is. You have a sound (your guitar DI, a vocal, a drum bus). You have a recording of a system — a cabinet, a room, a piece of hardware. Convolution plays your sound "through" that system and gives you back the result.

The intuition

Imagine clapping once in a cathedral. You hear the clap, then the reflections bouncing off the walls, the high ceiling, the stone floor — all smeared out over several seconds. That whole response is the room's "fingerprint" for a single clap. If you could record that fingerprint, you could take any other sound — a voice, a violin — and make it sound like it was performed in that cathedral. That is convolution.

The same idea works for a guitar cabinet. A speaker cabinet does not just amplify your guitar; it colors it with resonances, frequency response, and the particular way that speaker cone moves. Record the cabinet's response to a tiny click, and you can apply that exact coloring to any dry guitar signal.

Where you already use it

Cabinet Simulation

The classic use. A dry DI guitar goes in, a miked-cabinet sound comes out. The IR is the speaker, the cabinet, the microphone, and the mic position all baked into one file.

Convolution Reverb

Instead of an algorithm approximating a room, you use a recording of a real space — a concert hall, a plate, a spring tank. The result is the actual acoustics of that space.

Analog Hardware Emulation

EQ curves, transformer color, tape saturation, console summing — any linear piece of gear can be captured as an IR and applied with convolution.

Key idea: Convolution is linear and time-invariant. In plain terms: it colors your sound without distorting it (no added harmonics the way a distortion pedal would), and the coloring does not change over time. That is exactly what you want for cabs, rooms, and clean EQ — and exactly why it is the wrong tool for distortion or compression.

2. Impulse Responses — The Sonic DNA

An impulse response (IR) is a recording of a system's reaction to a very short, sharp input — usually a click, a sine sweep, or a starter pistol. That recording captures everything about how the system colors sound: its frequency response, its resonances, how long it rings, and the early reflections around it.

Think of an IR as the sonic DNA of a cabinet, room, or piece of gear. Feed a dry signal through that DNA and it comes out sounding like it went through the real thing.

The main types of IR

TypeWhat it capturesTypical lengthUse it for

Where to find them

  • Free libraries: Guitar Hack, God's Cab, Seacow, and many more shared by the community.
  • Commercial libraries: Celestion, Redwirez, York Audio, and others — usually recorded in treated rooms with high-end mics.
  • DIY: Play a click or sine sweep through your own cabinet, record it with a mic, and you have a custom IR of your exact rig.
Length matters: A shorter IR (0.5 s) gives a tighter, more focused sound with less low-end rumble. A longer IR (2 s) captures more of the cabinet's resonance and the room around it. For tight rhythm guitar, reach short. For ambience and depth, reach long.

3. FFT vs Direct Convolution — Why It Is 5000× Faster

Convolution is the right tool, but the obvious way to do it is painfully slow. The clever way — the one TaraDSP uses — is thousands of times faster. Here is why.

The slow way: direct convolution

Direct convolution works sample by sample. For every single output sample, you multiply it against every sample of the impulse response and add up the results. For a one-second file at 44.1 kHz convolved with a one-second IR, that is roughly 44,100 × 44,100 ≈ 2 billion multiply-add operations. For a whole song, it is completely impractical.

The fast way: the convolution theorem

Here is the trick that makes everything fast. A mathematician's theorem says:

Convolution in the time domain = Multiplication in the frequency domain

In plain language: instead of doing billions of little multiply-adds, you can:

  1. Transform both signals to the frequency domain using the FFT (Fast Fourier Transform). This is like looking at the sound as a recipe of frequencies instead of a waveform.
  2. Multiply them together — one simple multiply per frequency bin, instead of billions of operations.
  3. Transform back to the time domain with an inverse FFT. You have your convolved audio.

The FFT reduces the work from O(n²) to O(n log n). In practice, for audio-length signals, that is around a 5000× speedup. A convolution that would take minutes now takes milliseconds.

Why you should care: This is why TaraDSP can convolve a full song with a two-second room IR essentially instantly, and why real-time cab sim plugins can run on stage without melting your laptop. The FFT is the reason convolution is practical at all.

4. PFFFT — The FFT Engine Under the Hood

TaraDSP does not use a generic FFT. It uses PFFFT — "Pretty Fast FFT" — a library built specifically for audio. Two things make it special.

Real FFT = 2× faster

Audio samples are real numbers, not complex ones. A generic FFT still does the full complex math. PFFFT has a dedicated real FFT that skips the imaginary half of the work — roughly twice as fast for the exact kind of data audio is made of.

SIMD acceleration

Modern CPUs can do several calculations in a single instruction (SSE on Intel/AMD, NEON on Apple Silicon and ARM). PFFFT is written to use these, processing 4 to 8 samples per instruction. It is the difference between a polite engine and a very fast one.

The practical upshot: PFFFT is why TaraDSP stays fast across every platform it runs on — Windows, macOS (Intel and Apple Silicon), and Linux (x86_64 and ARM). The same optimized engine, tuned for real audio, everywhere.

Under the hood: TaraDSP uses PFFFT's internal transform format (not the "ordered" display format) because the convolution routine expects it. This is a programmer detail, but it is the kind of thing that, if gotten wrong, produces quietly broken audio — which is exactly why the manual calls it out.

5. Minimum Phase vs Linear Phase — Pre-Ringing Explained

This is the one concept that audibly changes your tone, so it is worth two minutes of your time.

What pre-ringing is

Some impulse responses are linear phase: their energy is symmetric — spread evenly before and after the main impulse. The energy that shows up before the main hit is called pre-ringing.

Why does that matter? Because in the real world, sound never arrives before the thing that made it. A guitar pick hits a string, then the sound reaches your ear. Pre-ringing puts a faint "ghost" of the sound ahead of the transient, and our ears hear that as unnatural — a smeared, slightly metallic or "phasey" quality, especially on drums and picked guitar.

What minimum phase does

A minimum phase IR rearranges the same frequency response so that all the energy sits at and after the main impulse. The result:

  • No pre-ringing — energy only arrives after the transient, like it does in nature.
  • Lower latency — the response is more compact, which matters for real-time playing.
  • Same frequency response — the tonal balance (the magnitude) is unchanged. Only the time alignment is different.
Linear phaseMinimum phase
Energy timingBefore AND after the impulseAt and after the impulse only
Pre-ringingYes (symmetric)No
LatencyHigherLower

Best forSome reverb, spacious/ambient soundsCabinet sim, real-time, tight rhythm
Rule of thumb: For cabinet simulation, always use minimum phase (--min). It is how real speakers and cabs behave, and it removes the pre-ringing that makes linear-phase cabs sound "not quite real." For reverb, either can work — some engineers prefer the spaciousness of linear phase for ambient pads.

6. Mastering Dither — Why 16-bit Needs It

Dither is one of the most misunderstood steps in digital audio, and also one of the easiest to get wrong. Here is the whole story in plain language.

The problem

Every digital audio file has to round its samples to the nearest available value — that rounding is called quantization. When you go from a high-resolution file (24-bit or 32-bit float) down to 16-bit, the rounding gets coarse. Without help, that coarse rounding turns quiet, fading details (a cymbal decay, reverb tail, the last breath of a held note) into harmonic distortion — a gritty, crunchy artifact that is very audible on exactly the material you least want it on.

The solution: TPDF dither

TPDF stands for "Triangular Probability Density Function." In plain terms: before rounding to 16-bit, TaraDSP adds a tiny, carefully-shaped layer of random noise. That noise is triangular — the sum of two random values — which is the ideal shape for this job.

The effect is that the distortion gets converted into a constant, low-level noise floor instead. A steady, quiet hiss is far less audible and far more natural than gritty harmonic distortion. This is the same principle behind why a vinyl record's surface noise is less objectionable than digital clipping.

Error feedback

TaraDSP goes one step further with error feedback. After each sample is rounded, the rounding error (the difference between the ideal value and the rounded value) is measured and fed back into the next sample. This shapes where the noise sits in the frequency spectrum, pushing it away from the range where our ears are most sensitive. The result is even cleaner than plain TPDF.

Do not dither twice. Dither is a one-time, final-output step. If you dither, then process, then dither again, you stack noise on noise and undo the benefit. Dither once, at the very end, only when going to 16-bit.
When to use it: Always dither for 16-bit output. Never dither for 24-bit or 32-bit — those formats have enough resolution that dither is unnecessary and only adds noise. In TaraDSP, dither engages automatically when you choose -b 16.

7. Resampling — Aliasing and Sample Rates

Sample rate is how many snapshots of the audio are taken per second. A 44.1 kHz file takes 44,100 snapshots a second; a 96 kHz file takes 96,000. Resampling is converting between these rates — for example, when your project runs at 48 kHz but a reverb IR was recorded at 44.1 kHz.

Why sample rate matters

A sample rate can only represent frequencies up to half its value — a limit called the Nyquist frequency. For 44.1 kHz audio, that is 22.05 kHz, comfortably above the range of human hearing. This is why 44.1 kHz became the CD standard: it captures everything we can hear.

Aliasing — the artifact to avoid

When you downsample (say, 96 kHz to 44.1 kHz) without filtering first, any frequencies above the new Nyquist limit do not politely disappear. They fold back into the audible range as inharmonic, metallic garbage. This is called aliasing, and it is the digital equivalent of a fax machine screaming.

Good resamplers avoid aliasing by applying a steep anti-aliasing filter before downsampling — they remove the frequencies that would fold back, so nothing lands in your audio as an artifact.

Engine quality in TaraDSP

TaraDSP picks the best available resampling engine, in this order of preference:

  1. SoX Resampler (libsoxr) — very high quality, proper anti-aliasing. The best option; install libsoxr for it.
  2. R8Brain — very high quality, used when libsoxr is unavailable.
  3. FinalCD — an external tool, used when present.
  4. Linear interpolation — the always-available fallback. It works, but it has no anti-aliasing, so use it only when nothing else is available.
Practical tip: If you care about the final quality, install libsoxr so TaraDSP uses the SoX engine. It is the difference between a transparent sample-rate conversion and one that can leave subtle metallic artifacts on cymbals and bright transients.

8. IR Combination — Merging Two IRs Into One

Sometimes one IR is not enough. You want a cabinet and the room it sits in. Or a cabinet with an EQ curve baked in. Or a blend of two different speakers. TaraDSP can merge two impulse responses into a single new IR, and the reason it works comes down to one elegant property of convolution.

Why it works: associativity

Convolution is associative. In plain language: if you take a signal and run it through IR X, then through IR Y, the result is identical to running it through a single IR that is itself the combination of X and Y. The order of grouping does not matter.

Signal → (IR X → IR Y)  =  Signal → (IR X * IR Y)

So to combine two IRs, you simply convolve one IR with the other. The output is a brand-new WAV that behaves as a single IR containing both. Use it anywhere you would use a normal IR.

What you can build

Cab + Room

Combine a close-miked cab IR with a room IR to get a "cabinet in a room" sound — the dry speaker tone plus the space around it, in one file.

Cab + EQ

Convolve a cab IR with an EQ curve (as an IR) to bake the EQ permanently into the cabinet. The result is a new cab IR with that tonal shape built in.

Two Cabs Blended

Mix two different speakers — say a 4×12 and a 2×12 — into a single IR. The relative loudness of each depends on the source levels, so normalize first if needed.

How to do it in TaraDSP

Treat one IR as the input signal and the other as the impulse response. The output is your combined IR:

# Blend a close-miked cab with a room IR
TaraDSP -x cab_close.wav -y cab_room.wav -o cab_blend.wav -b 32 --min
# Bake an EQ curve into a cab IR
TaraDSP -x cab_ir.wav -y eq_curve.wav -o cab_eq.wav -b 32 --min
Always combine at 32-bit float (-b 32). This preserves the full dynamic range with no quantization loss. Only reduce to 16- or 24-bit for the final release, after all processing is done.
Use --min when combining for cabinet work. It removes pre-ringing at each stage and keeps the combined IR tight and natural.
